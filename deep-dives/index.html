A path that turns reading into skill

Choose one consequential feature to work through all three modules. A nutrition-information draft assistant, multilingual job-post helper or internal support copilot works well. Keep your first exercise synthetic and draft-only so you can test failure cases without affecting users. These are proposed extensions inspired by familiar project types, not claims about the current state of any repository.

  1. Define the work before choosing autonomy. Write one user journey, its current baseline, who is affected by failure and who may authorize a consequential action. Domain 6 supplies the discovery brief; it is useful to skim that template before reading Domain 5.
  2. Draw the trust and data boundaries. Follow input, retrieval, prompts, tools, logs and human decisions. Name where each control actually runs. Complete Domain 5’s risk and reviewer exercises.
  3. Turn expectations into acceptance evidence. Choose constraints that must all pass, then optimization goals. Record an ADR and a measurable service contract in Domain 6. Make the denominator, window, exclusions and owner explicit.
  4. Build a repeatable team workflow. Apply Domain 7’s configuration distinctions and offline debugging lab. Keep policy enforcement, runtime isolation and human instructions separate. Collect evidence that another developer can reproduce.
  5. Review the assembled system. Explain each trade-off to a technical peer and a product stakeholder. Use the capstone below to find missing ownership, stale approval, hidden review load or an untested rollback.
  6. Use checks as diagnosis. Answer the ten scenarios per module before expanding rationales. A correct choice is useful only if you can explain why the other choices fail under that scenario’s conditions.

Read in several sessions rather than a single skim. For every worked example, change one assumption—traffic, user population, action impact, data rights or recovery requirement—and identify which decision must change. Suggested lesson times include explanation and short reflection; running a real pilot takes additional project time.

One feature, three connected responsibilities

Capstone scenario: a marketplace team proposes a multilingual assistant that drafts job descriptions and suggests matches. The first pilot may prepare suggestions for an authorized person; it cannot rank workers automatically for exclusion, alter wages, send offers or move money. Domain 5: decide what must be protected

Create a data inventory and risk register. Test indirect injection in job text, unauthorized records, misleading wage statements, stale approvals and disparate burden across languages. Define review authority and an explicit stop path. Successful parsing is not evidence that a suggestion is appropriate or authorized.

Domain 6: decide what success and ownership mean

Record an approved scope, accountable release owner and measurable pilot criteria. Separate usefulness, latency, appeal access and reviewer load. Agree who may change scope, who handles complaints and what evidence is needed to expand beyond drafts.

Domain 7: make the implementation reproducible

Pin the evaluation bundle, document development commands, scope tool permissions and test runtime access separately. Reproduce a failure with synthetic fixtures, ship a reviewed repair and prove rollback and handoff using the receiving team’s actual access.

A pilot scorecard that resists misleading averages

Suppose 1,000 synthetic or consented pilot tasks include 800 in language A and 200 in language B. The assistant completes 760 A tasks and 140 B tasks. Overall completion is 900/1,000 = 90%, but the slices are 95% and 70%. If the team agreed an 85% minimum completion gate for each language, the release fails that independent gate. A higher overall target does not repair the disparity. Diagnose task mix, data quality, language support and review routing before changing a threshold.

Now suppose 25% of 100 hourly requests need a reviewer, each review takes six minutes, and two reviewers are available. Demand is 25 reviews/hour; nominal capacity is 2 × 60/6 = 20. The queue grows by five cases per hour before accounting for breaks or bursts. Do not fix the promised response time by silently reducing mandatory review. Reduce accepted demand, add trained capacity or negotiate a narrower approved pilot.

For a separate event-based draft-availability objective of 99% over 10,000 eligible tasks, allowance is 100 bad events. Recording 75 bad events leaves 25. That availability result cannot offset a missed language gate, unsafe action, privacy breach or overloaded reviewer. Every figure here is an invented teaching assumption; each project must justify its own definitions and thresholds.

The practical proof package

  1. Discovery evidence: one observed workflow, a baseline, affected users, assumptions, exclusions and a person who can decide scope.
  2. Control evidence: a data-flow map, risk owners, executor checks, denial behavior, review policy and representative adversarial cases.
  3. Decision evidence: a short ADR comparing feasible alternatives and naming the assumption that would trigger reconsideration.
  4. Quality evidence: a versioned evaluation set with slice denominators, failure severities, expected outcomes and acknowledged blind spots.
  5. Operating evidence: sanitized trace examples, on-call ownership, a tested fallback, explicit rollback conditions and a release record tying configuration to results.
  6. Handoff evidence: a second person can set up the environment, run the checks, identify an incident and execute the approved recovery path without borrowing the author’s credentials.

The templates give you a starting point for these artifacts. Completing a document is not the acceptance test: try the denied action, let approval become stale, remove a dependency, introduce a repeat request, change the population mix and ask the receiving team to recover the system.

Translate practical skill into exam judgment

The supplied Professional guide assigns 14%, 14% and 7% to these domains, together 35% of its blueprint. Its 13 objective bullets define this collection’s scope. Local labels such as 5.1 identify study mappings; the guide itself does not number each bullet.

In a scenario, first locate the lifecycle phase and the unresolved obligation. A technically strong implementation can still be premature if the task’s success criteria, data rights or decision authority are unknown. A later monitoring improvement cannot substitute for an executor permission check. A larger model cannot supply a missing approval, and a human checkbox cannot repair a review process without evidence, time and authority.

Look for independent constraints, not merely the highest metric. Distinguish preventive control from detection, a provider’s capabilities from the customer system’s obligations, a proposal from the observed outcome, an SLO from a contractual SLA, and a source-code worktree from an isolation boundary. The decision checks require that same reasoning without reproducing the live item bank.

The collection supports preparation; it does not establish live-exam calibration or guarantee a score. The six original mock forms remain a separate practice collection in the repository, with their own source and scoring disclosures.
